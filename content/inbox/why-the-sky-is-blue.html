Why the sky is blue

22 November 2026 3 min read inbox

In short

Air molecules scatter short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones, so sunlight bounced sideways across the sky arrives at your eye loaded with blue. The scattering strength follows a fourth-power law, which also explains why the sky is not violet and why the horizon fades to white.

The short answer

Sunlight looks white but carries every visible wavelength, from roughly 400 nm at the violet end to about 750 nm at the red end [1][3]. Most of that light passes straight through the atmosphere. A small fraction is redirected by the gas molecules it meets, and that redirection is Rayleigh scattering: elastic scattering from particles much smaller than the wavelength of the light [2].

The scattered intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength, so shorter waves are deflected far more often than longer ones [2]. The light reaching you from a patch of sky away from the Sun is exactly this sideways-scattered sunlight, and it is therefore enriched in blue [1].

Worked example

Rayleigh scattering makes intensity fall as the fourth power of wavelength [2]. For two wavelengths, the ratio of scattered intensity is (λlong / λshort)4. Using visible wavelengths [3]:

  • 400 nm vs 700 nm: (700/400)4 ≈ 9.4 — violet is scattered nearly ten times as strongly as deep red.
  • 450 nm vs 700 nm: (700/450)4 ≈ 5.9 — blue versus deep red.
  • 450 nm vs 650 nm: (650/450)4 ≈ 4.4 — blue versus the red used in many textbook examples.
  • 400 nm vs 450 nm: (450/400)4 ≈ 1.6 — violet versus blue.

That last line is the whole problem: by the law alone, the zenith should be violet, not blue.

Why the sky is not violet

The violet question is old, and the standard resolution is not one effect but three [2]:

  • The Sun is not a flat lamp. Sunlight delivers less energy in the violet than in the blue, so there is less violet to scatter in the first place [2][3].
  • The eye is not a flat detector. Human sensitivity falls off sharply toward the violet end, so light at 400 nm looks dimmer than light at 450 nm even at equal intensity [2][3].
  • Some violet never arrives. Part of the short-wavelength light is absorbed high in the atmosphere before it can be scattered down to you [3].

Sources weigh the third factor differently. NOAA's explainer presents absorption in the upper atmosphere as a co-equal reason for the missing violet [3]. The encyclopedic treatment attributes the daylight colour mainly to the solar spectrum and to human vision, and notes that ozone absorption matters most around twilight rather than at midday [2][4]. The first two factors are not in dispute.

The result is not a pure spectral blue. The daytime sky spectrum is broad, with a maximum around 450–485 nm, and the perceived colour matches a blue near 474–476 nm mixed with white light — an unsaturated blue rather than a saturated one [4].

Where the simple story changes

"Shorter scatters more" describes a clear sky seen away from the Sun. To predict the rest of the sky you have to add path length and repeated scattering.

Near the horizon you are looking through far more air than at the zenith. Along that longer path, blue light is scattered many times, and each scattering event can send it in any direction. The multiple scattering mixes the wavelengths back together, so the sky pales toward white as it approaches the horizon [1][3].

At sunset the geometry does the opposite. Sunlight reaches you through a long slant path, most of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam, and what survives is the orange and red end [1][3]. Same physics, different path length.

Ozone returns at twilight: absorption in its Chappuis band helps maintain the blue of the evening sky [4]. And scattered skylight is linearly polarised, which is why a polarising filter darkens one part of the sky more than another [2].
