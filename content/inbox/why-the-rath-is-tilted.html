Why the rath is tilted

27 November 2026 3 min read inbox

In short

Rath is the chariot in Nepal's Rato Machhindranath festival: a roughly 60-foot wooden tower pulled through Lalitpur on four wheels. It tilts because its slender timber frame can shift or crack under the strain of pulling. The 2015 earthquake left it leaning for months, and a bad lean stops the procession until the frame is repaired.

What "rath" means here

The festival's Nepali name, रथयात्रा (rathayātrā), contains the word rath — the chariot [3]. The rath in question is the one built for Rato Machhindranath, the red rain god of Lalitpur (Patan). Each spring the chariot is built at Pulchowk: about 60 feet tall, made from carved wood and rope [3]. The Rubin Museum puts it at around 10 tons and notes that no nails or screws are used [4]. The idol is placed inside, and teams haul the tower through Gabahal, Mangal Bazar, Sundhara and Lagankhel to Jawalakhel, where the Bhoto Jatra ceremony closes the month-long procession [3].

The sources describe the construction cycle a little differently. Wikipedia describes a fresh 60-foot chariot at Pulchowk each year plus a separate chariot built at Bungamati every 12 years for the "Barha Barsa Jatra" [3]. The Rubin Museum says the chariot is rebuilt from scratch every 12 years [4]. Both agree on the essentials: the structure is temporary, handmade, and very tall for its base.

Why the wooden tower leans

The chariot's core is four lansi: 36-foot timber pillars about 10 cm in diameter, tied at the four corners and wrapped in reeds. That bound bundle, rather than a rigid chassis, carries the load [1]. When the ropes are hauled, the load passes through those pillars; one that slips or snaps leaves the whole frame out of plumb.

The 2007 season shows the mechanism. After the chariot had been pulled only about 100 metres from Pulchowk, it tilted to the right and the locals stopped the procession. A chariot construction expert suspected that the lansi had been displaced or broken, and said the outer layer would have to be opened up to inspect them [1]. The repair was not cosmetic: if the pillars were damaged, the finial had to come down and the idol had to be removed, and once the idol was out the work had to be finished within 15 days [1].

It has failed more violently. In 2000 the chariot toppled and the Bhoto Jatra had to be postponed [2]; in 2004 it went over at Sundhara and injured eight people [1]. In 2015 the earthquake made the chariot sway from side to side; it did not fall, but it was left leaning at Chyasikot, needed structural repairs, and halted the 12-year Bungamati–Patan procession into July [2]. The pattern is consistent: the rath is not meant to lean. A lean is a report that the frame has moved.

What happens after a tilt

When the chariot leans, the procession stops and the structure is treated as suspect until it has been inspected. The 2007 response is a useful template:

  • Stop pulling and secure the chariot where it stands.
  • Strip the reed skin and inspect the four lansi.
  • If a pillar is displaced or broken, take down the finial and remove the idol.
  • Repair or rebuild, then resume the route.

The stakes are not only engineering. The Nepali Times notes that myths of doom have long surrounded any disruption of Machhindranath's journey, which is why a visible tilt is news in Patan rather than a routine maintenance note [2].
