Why Earth is tilted

27 November 2026 3 min read inbox

In short

Earth's rotation axis leans about 23.44° from perpendicular to its orbital plane. The leading explanation is a collision with a Mars-sized body about 4.5 billion years ago, the same event that formed the Moon. The lean is not fixed: it drifts between 22.1° and 24.5° over a 41,000-year cycle, and the Moon keeps that drift small.

The short answer

Axial tilt, or obliquity, is the angle between a planet's rotation axis and its orbital axis, the line perpendicular to the plane of its orbit [4]. Earth's tilt is about 23.44° [1][4]. Britannica describes the value as measured “relative to the plane of the orbit” [1]; the stricter definition measures it from the perpendicular [4]. The number is the same, but the two phrasings are not interchangeable.

The leading explanation is the giant-impact hypothesis, which is also the standard account of the Moon's origin [1][3]. About 4.5 billion years ago, a body roughly the size of Mars — usually called Theia — struck the young Earth at an angle. The impact shifted Earth's axis and flung molten and vaporised rock into orbit, where it gathered into the Moon [1][3]. NASA is careful about the details: the impactor may have been one object or several [3], and the formation models are still being refined.

The same collision left Earth spinning much faster than it does today. The Moon recedes about 1.5 inches (3.8 cm) a year, which implies it formed closer and that early Earth days were shorter [3].

Why the lean survives

A spinning body keeps the direction of its axis unless a torque acts on it, so Earth's pole points at the same part of the sky all year while the planet orbits the Sun — a property called axial parallelism [4].

The Moon keeps the angle within narrow limits. Its gravity raises tidal bulges, and those bulges increase Earth's rotational inertia, making the axis harder to tip [1]. Over the past million years the tilt has varied between 22.1° and 24.5° on a cycle of about 41,000 years [2]. It is currently about 23.4° and decreasing, heading for a minimum roughly 10,000 years from now [2], at a measured rate of about 46.8 arcseconds per century [4].

Mars shows what Earth avoids. Without a large moon, its obliquity is thought to have swung between 0° and 60° over millions of years, possibly chaotically [4]. Some researchers dispute the chaotic label, but the wider range is not in dispute [4].

What the tilt does

The tilt is what makes seasons. As Earth orbits, one pole leans toward the Sun while the other leans away, so each hemisphere gets a higher or lower noon Sun and longer or shorter days [2][4]. Distance is not the cause: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during the northern winter [2].

Worked example

Noon Sun height above the horizon is approximately 90° − |latitude − declination|. At the solstices the Sun's declination is about ±23.44° [4]. For London, at 51.5°N:

  • June solstice: 90° − (51.5 − 23.44) ≈ 61.9°
  • December solstice: 90° − (51.5 + 23.44) ≈ 15.1°

So the same city sees the noon Sun climb almost 47° between the two solstices.

The same 23.44° fixes the tropics at 23.44° north and south of the equator, and the polar circles at 66.56°.
